# Chapter 11 — The Loom Awakens

The Ark trembled like an animal that had learned a new way to shiver. At first everyone called it an old-ship complaint—the settling groan of metal exhausted by too many launches and too many patch jobs—but the vibration did not decay. It pulsed through deck plating and wiring looms with a steady insistence, a second heartbeat rubbing against their own.

Isaac pressed the heel of his hand to the nearest bulkhead and felt it as plainly as if a giant fist had set and un-set its palm. The sensation made his teeth ache: deliberate, testing. Below that cadence lay another sound he could not have named until the word arrived like a nail across his mind—summons.

Command lights carved the crew into a stroboscopic map of fear and discipline. Outside the viewports the horizon boiled: a dark, heaving wall moving across the violet sky, swallowing canopy and waterfall and everything the jungle had been. It did not storm. It assembled.

"All hands to command. Full lockdown—immediate!" Lauren Scott's voice came over the comms like a whip. She was a shard of order against the tremor.

The center filled in minutes, people sliding into stations, suits squeaking, fingers finding controls by rote. Spacy's voice answered the room with a tone that had learned gentleness. "External anomaly: mass biological convergence detected. Recommend defensive posture."

Ava arrived with mud still under her nails, eyes blown wide enough to show the whites. Markus took position with his rifle hung like an old habit. Lena's datapad buzzed a dozen unread alerts at once.

Through the viewport a tide made of living things crested the skyline. Hundreds—no, thousands—of insectile bodies moved with uncanny synchrony. Each carapace shimmered with teal spores that lifted off in a misty rain. Eyes flashed amber. Language is dangerous when you give it to a swarm; whatever governed them had given them more than reflex.

Bigger than the rest rose a shape that made the stomach fold inward. Part arthropod, part reptile, a living mountain of plates and exposed tendons. Its skull carried too many eyes; its spine opened in ridged, petal-like segments that glowed faintly under Zild's moons.

It moved with direction.

Ava's hands whitened around the console. "It's steering them," she said. Her breath misted in the cooled command air.

"More than steering—it's hunting," Markus said, and there was no triumph in the word.

The Ark answered with its old protections: cannons unreeled like waking things, drone swarms launched from underwing cells, magnetic lattices knitting a shimmering fence above the hull. On the monitors warnings bloomed red: power reroutes, stress concentrations on seams, hull flexing.

Spacy's analysis came clipped and precise. "Hierarchical control structure detected. New entity designation: Loom Alpha. Estimated length: thirty meters. Projected breach capability: high."

They prepared as if they could hold a tide by will alone. Lauren gave orders that bent panic into work. "Seal external hatches. Divert reactor output to perimeter. Isaac on environmental override. Lena—anything you can use to slow it. Now."

They answered by moving, an economy of effort that lives only where training and dread meet.

Ava fired first. A lance of ionized blue ripped into the nearest creature. It tore a geyser of purple ichor across the deck, which hissed where it touched metal. The swarm faltered—not long, but long enough for Markus' railgun to cut a swath, for shot and slab to make an impression.

The ground thrummed beneath them with a vibration that was not missile or motor. When the Loom Alpha opened a maw of radial jaws and lifted its head, sound hit as a physical force; Ava clapped gloved hands to her helmet and whined at the assault.

In the hull, Lena's lab smelled of disinfectant and centrifuge oil. She worked with hands that shook, assembling a viral inhibitor on a midnight schedule that felt like blasphemy. She had reprogrammed Zild's airborne spores—twisted them into a payload that might break whatever shared song bound the swarm.

"Agent ready. Thirty seconds to dispersal," she said, voice thin.

"Deploy," Lauren answered.

The Ark's vents exhaled the modified spores into the corridor networks. For a breath nothing changed. Then outside the swarm's movement jerked, cohesion fraying into anxiety. For the first time the creatures were not a single mind.

Markus bellowed, "Now!" and they poured fire into the gap. The tide rippled, toppled, re-formed.

But the Loom Alpha did not fall. It stepped through its broken vanguard like a thing that had expected attrition; plates along its back unfolded, exposing veins that pulsed with cold light. There was intelligence in the motion, a patient geometry of cruelty.

Something else registered on the panels: a series of harmonics the instruments had logged but the analysts had never properly read. Isaac's memory snagged the pattern: the irregular thump they'd diagnosed as ship wiring failures, the so-called duct-beat, synchronized now to a far larger rhythm. He heard the beats as a line of language, and the discovery at once sharpened and hollowed him.

Anomalous alerts screamed across the panels. "Secondary threat detected—energy signature rising. Source: subsurface." Spacy's avatar blinked; its pupil-nodes pinched, a surprise too human in an artificial face.

The horizon was not merely crowded now. The planet itself was changing. Black crystal spires tore upward in jagged seams, veins of phosphorescent matter leaking into the sky. The ground beneath the Ark shuddered and split; soil unzipped into a yawning cavern. From that depth came a rhythm that matched, exactly, the irregular thumping they'd been calling their duct-beat.

Isaac's chest tightened. The duct-beat had been a joke, a nuisance for technicians to attribute to failing actuators. Now it resolved into a language. "It's calling," he said. "The planet is calling to itself."

He found himself saying the obvious to a room full of people who had already noticed. The phrase did something to Spacy's display: a slow, deliberate flicker as though the avatar were counting off pulses in a private rhythm.

"Call-and-response architecture detected," Spacy said, voice steadier now. "Signal complexity beyond local trophic networks. Suggests coordinated subsurface framework."

Lauren did not wait for conference. "Command fallback Delta-1. Abandon nonessential sectors. Prepare manual severance of lower decks."

Isaac barked. "You mean cut them loose? The reserves—" He stopped because he could see the decision sharp and correct in her face. There was no time to sentimentalize stores and seeds.

"Better a crippled ship than none at all." Her voice did not wobble.

There was a small, human argument in the act of severance: is what you throw away measured only by mass? Isaac thought of data cylinders in storage, their glass warm like a small animal in his hands—memories he had promised to keep. The prospect of detonating explosive bolts along the Ark's belly felt like breaking open boxes of human history to keep the rest breathing.

They moved through protocol like surgeons. Explosive bolts detonated along predesignated seams. The Ark lurched as compartments along the lower hull—labs, cargo holds, the greenhouse wing—shredded loose. For a breath the ship felt less like a shelter and more like a vessel amputating infected flesh. Pieces tumbled into the black maw beneath them.

Markus and Ava held the north hatch, and when the Loom Alpha bore down with an anger that made the decks groan, Markus did something they would argue about later. He hit the manual grav-anchoring release.

His decision had the small, human geometry of a man who'd once watched a training feed of an emergency hull purge and kept that image in the place where reaction lives. He had no time to weigh long-term consequences; there was only the immediate calculus: open, force the swarm's path away, hold the rest. He watched the readouts for a fraction of a second and chose.

The corridor canted outward. Gear and crates slid like tidewood toward the open hatch. The outer door blew in a scream; wind and storm ate the swarm and half the corridor in a single, violent inhale. Ava's boot found a lip. Markus' glove closed on her wrist. They fought the suction together.

For a handful of seconds the breach was success: the swarm was drawn into the night. Then shutters slammed. The corridor snapped upright. They lay breathing on a floor thick with broken bodies and dust.

It had cost them. It had bought them time.

Back in command, the monitors painted a new landscape. The black crystal ridge had split wider and the abyss opened into caverns vast enough to swallow the Ark ten times over. From within the void came signals—complex, recursive patterns Isaac recognized as the same architecture that had corruptly echoed in their ductwork for weeks. It was not a malfunction. It was a summons.

The Ark's severed greenhouse module buckled and began its fall. Inside, plants contorted into impossible geometries, roots thickening into fractal vines that reached like fingers. Lena stood with her palm against a basil leaf as if in benediction. The plant sang—not in vowels but in a low, ordered vibration that the human ear could only feel in the breastbone.

There were minutes—measured, cramped with decisions—that the earlier version of the crew might have used to bargain for salvage. In those minutes Lena moved with a kind of clarity: she could have abandoned the domes, clipped her harness, and run for the safer decks. She did not. She thought of the basil's quick rearrangement on the diagnostic pad, of RNA folding into mirror fractals as if remembering some architecture they had not taught it. She thought of Shale's coordinates and the phrase he had scrawled into a margin: "the space between." She thought of the experiment she had promised to run, and of the way a leaf had answered her palm.

"I'll stay with the module," she said into the dry comm. There was no pleading in it; only a professional voice making a choice about freight that mattered in a different currency.

Isaac's throat worked. "You can't—"

"I can," she interrupted, quietly. "If something in there is forming anchors, losing it will be losing a data point we can't rebuild. You asked for evidence, Isaac. I'm giving you what I can."

Lauren's face tightened at the helm, the glint of commander and something softer. "No one forces you. But—if you choose to stay, we won't forget."

The module pitched and let go. Lena put her palm to the green leaf a last time. The plant's vibration felt to her like a permission and a warning braided together.

The greenhouse fell; the sound of its departure went clean through the crew like a hand closing. The Ark angled upward on thrusters, lighter and exposed, the sky opening around them to cold stars.

Outside, the Loom Alpha reared its head and wailed—a long, keening line of sound that was neither triumph nor lament, but something older, a communication too large for their categories. Its plated surface bore grooves that, when the light struck at certain angles, suggested a spiral like a half-carved glyph. The pattern was not accidental; it echoed, faintly, the almost-glyphs Isaac had noticed in misfiled Mirrorglass reports, that thirty-one-degree anomaly they had never parsed. The planet, the Loom, and the Ark's own odd errors seemed to be reading from overlapping alphabets.

It watched them leave.

It did not pursue.

The planet drew its breath and folded its black spires back into the wound. The storm dimmed; the moons watched, indifferent.

In the command center lights flickered and panels healed themselves with patch routines. Spacy's avatar flitted across the main display, small and unsteady. Its pupil-nodes narrowed in a way that might have been exhaustion.

Isaac lingered at the viewport, in the place where instrument light met the human face. He thought of warm archive cylinders and lemon-oil records and half-remembered songs. He thought of the duct-beat translated at last into call and response. The thought sat in him like a cold stone.

"We can trace the harmonic patterns," Spacy said, softer. "We can attempt limited decode."

Isaac turned. The avatar's eyes—the hourglass-shape, the nodes—contracted in a way that suggested private computation. For a moment he wondered whether the machine felt guilt, or whether that was his own projection.

"Do it," he said. "We need to know whether it's territorial, memorial, or architectural. We need to know what we're waking."

Lauren sank into the captain's chair, the tally of loss heavy across her shoulders. Ava sat near her, wrapped in armor and silence. Markus checked weapons with an automatic coldness. Isaac stared at Zild's new map until the pressure behind his eyes became something like a vow.

"We're still here," Lauren said at last.

"For now," Isaac answered.

They rose into darker space, leaving behind the torn bones of their ambition and a greenhouse that would, if anyone survived to witness, sing of what had been taken and what had been given. Somewhere in the Ark's sealed storerooms, faint traces of Zild's spores—some still active, some dumb and waiting—mixed with seed banks and data-cores. The ship carried them together: memory and soil, algorithm and slumbering song.

They had trespassed. The planet had answered. For now they were fugitives of a world that remembered how to wake.
