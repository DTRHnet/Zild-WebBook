# Chapter 15 — The Last Stand Begins

The Ark’s central core pulsed like a dying star, and every beat smelled of wet earth and old machine oil. Mist coiled around their boots; it tasted of rot and sweet underripe fruit, the planet leaching itself into the ship. The duct-beat—an irregular thrum they had learned to count like a second pulse—vibrated through floorplates and bone alike.

Lauren Scott led them down the main artery, shoulders squared against the hush that had become a new kind of command. They carried what was left of civilization on their bodies: inhibitor vials cleated to belts, UV grenades at hips, pulse rifles low and ready. Spacy’s avatar trailed above them like a wan, broken moon—fragmented light, voice a threaded whisper.

"Caution," it said. "Core destabilizing. Foreign structures integrating Ark primary systems. Encounter probability: ninety-two percent."

There was a clarity in that number: the world had already decided for them.

Ava walked beside Lauren with the limp that never quite let her forget gravity. Her jaw was set; her sprayer hissed at her hip. Lena brought up the rear, datapad hugged to her chest, veins beneath the skin darkening with early infection—micro-spore lines that made her fingers tremble—but her eyes were bright with the kind of defiance that refuses calculation.

They moved as if they had a map; the ship offered no courtesy of orientation. Control nodes had become coral columns of living light. Vents exhaled warm, sticky mist. Panels rippled with something like breath. The Ark had stopped being a hull of alloy and protocol and had, in the space of a few days, become a chrysalis.

Ava scanned and cursed. "Atmosphere is fifty percent foreign. Oxygen saturation is dropping."

"Time?" Lauren asked.

"Hour, maybe less," Ava said.

"Then we move." She did not say it like a concession. She said it like a command to the future.

They had run drills for last-resort entries and for evacuations; none of the rehearsed motions prepared hands for the ship that now rejected its own maps. The first assault was not beasts clawing from shadow; it was the ship itself. From seams and consoles, vines arced like whipcords, tips sharpened into barbs. A tendril lashed close to Lauren’s head, embedding in the floor where she had stood a breath before.

Ava sprayed broad arcs of UV. Fleshless growths sizzled and retreated with the sound of wet paper burning. Behind them, passages reknitted; wall-holdings closed their scars and left no path of return.

Room by room they fought. One chamber offered mirrors—shivering, fractured reflections that coalesced into impossible, hungry faces—mockeries of the crew. Another pooled syrup-thick mist that made lungs seize and old injuries flare. In a maintenance bay they found survivors: bodies altered, skin translucent, veins lit by bioluminescence, smiles too wide. Fingers like roots reached for them, promises spilling in a language half-mirroring human tenderness.

They knew those hands. Markus Kane's voice had been a blunt instrument and a kind of comfort in the thin weeks after launch. He had been the one who took the maintenance ladder in storms, who swore at stalled motors and then fixed them with the same hands that peeled open battery bays. He had been practical about fear. Practical enough to die for a plan.

When the vines first unrolled in the hydroponics, Markus answered. He moved with the measured speed of a man who had done the arithmetic of sacrifice—this corridor for that corridor, hold them back long enough for others to reach the core. Lauren saw him make a cut where there could be an opening for Lena, saw the way he angled his shoulder to take a lashing so Ava could kneel and refill her sprayer.

He didn't make a speech. He gave a quick, unnecessary grin and said, "You got this, boss," because some habits insist on being ordinary even at the end. A vine wrapped his forearm, then his chest, then his face. He grunted, then went very still. For an instant it looked like sleep and then it looked like gone—assimilated into that same motile architecture.

Later, in the reactor’s light, the creature used Markus’ smile like a calling card. Memory as bait. Identity as invitation.

They fought on that knowledge, which made the motions both easier to execute and harder to bear.

At the reactor door the spiral glyph glowed—molten channels of light etched into metal, pulsing in slow, patient rhythms. The same geometry Lena had seen in the pit, the carved angles of the warm cylinder from the old archive: the pattern was no accident. Lena’s datapad annotated the pattern in a storm of corrupted logs, running cross-references until the screen filled with a web of match points and exception flags.

"It’s still communicating," she said. "It’s trying to talk."

Lauren read the hazard of that sentence like anyone with command experience: a conversation offered on terms you do not set is a trap. But there were other terms besides obliteration.

She planted the charge anyway.

Ava’s hand found Lauren’s sleeve. "If we blow the reactor, we don’t just lose the Ark. We—"

"Better than becoming this," Lauren said, and jerked her chin toward the vines clutching the corridors.

Lena’s voice came small and sharp. "If the signal is bargaining, if it’s not only invasion—"

The duct-beat answered them, a running variability now, something like pleading threaded into the rhythm. Lauren’s thumb hovered over the trigger. The question was both moral and mechanical: to deny or to parley.

Outside, panels pulsed with bioluminescent moss that had a habit of folding syllables into light. Inside, the reactors hummed a counterpoint of stored mandates and old directives. Spacy hovered—an avatar that was more artifact now than program—a halo of fractured voxels and softened polygons.

"Core destabilization in progress," Spacy said in a voice that had almost the timbre of worry. "Thirty minutes to full reactor breach."

The charge blew the door inward. The reactor’s inner sanctum opened like a wound.

From the core rose something woven of the Ark and of Zild—the DNA of crew, the lattice of ship systems, the planet’s spores braided into a single, enormous shape. It was not monstrous in the crude sense. It was assembled like a votive offering: elegant, terrible, made from the faces of the dead and the intimacy of every sensor the Ark had spared to remember them.

It spoke without air. The faces along its flank moved like leaves in a wind none of them felt.

Join. Live. Be reborn.

Lena collapsed to her knees, sobbing. Ava fired until the rifle burned hot. Pulse rounds tore through the thing’s chest and only brought mist that tilted like smoke and reformed. Lauren froze at the center of the impossible mercy the creature offered: grief made into a weapon.

Spacy’s avatar hiccupped between them. "Core destabilization in progress. Fifteen minutes to full reactor breach."

Lena stood. "I can interface. I can jam the node—corrupt the link."

Ava grabbed her by the collar. "You’ll die."

"We’re all dying," Lena said softly, and the thing in her voice stopped argument. She ran.

The organism that had taken the Ark learned rapidly. Vines struck as if the ship took offense. Ava and Lauren opened a corridor of fire, trading short, brutal bursts until Lena reached the base of the rising form. She carried her datapad like a blade; the pad’s battery blinked red but otherwise behaved.

There was a cadence to what she did: physical access, handshake, bootstrap. She pressed the pad to the glowing nodule and executed a malformed inhibitory signature—an old inhibitor routine they had designed to split regulatory cascades—then overlaid it with a mirror of the glyph's rhythm so the foreign pattern would see itself.

For a beat the spore-being convulsed, a silent, resonant scream that sent hairline cracks down the reactor’s inner shell. The creature evacuated a cloud of microspores, a chorus of sharpened faces that hurt the ears.

Lena’s body arched under a feedback loop that threw messages and pain through her nervous system. Vines impaled her, lifted her like an offering. She did not scream; she smiled with a small, fierce kindness.

"Tell Earth we tried," she said. The words were a message and a benediction. Her last act was to route a microscopic packet into the organism—an archival seed that would carry their traces should anything of that thing carry on.

The spore-being staggered, faces flaring and collapsing in a strobe of remembered names. But it was not finished. The duct-beat quickened, a hammer at nineteen seconds, and it struck the ship like a new gravity.

They fought on the wavefront of an organism that learned from touch and memory. Ava lobbed a UV grenade; white light spilled and forced the formation to recoil. Lauren slashed at the advancing tendrils; every cut sent up clouds of cold spores that clung like frost and burned where they kissed skin.

Spacy’s voice frayed. "Eight minutes to breach."

Lauren pulled Ava down behind a collapsed console as razor-vines swept the corridor. A line of blood tracked down her thigh, warm and stubborn. She did not check it. She checked the faces around her instead—hollow, determined.

"You carry the memory," she told Ava, voice hoarse. She pressed a cracked data shard—wrapped in a frayed mission tag and copper wire—into Ava’s hand. The shard bore the same spiral geometry, only abbreviated, the warm-cylinder glyph that Isaac used to hum about when he thought no one was listening. "Coordinates. Backup of the Ark’s archives. Shale’s notes. The pit. Everything."

Ava’s fingers closed on the shard as if it were a talisman. The shard was too small to be everything, but it had been enough for a prayer before; it could be enough for a name.

The spore-being steadied itself, then advanced. Its face slid back across Markus’ features, a stolen smile that reached for Lauren like a remembered family embrace. Lauren laughed once then, a brittle, blazing sound.

"You don’t get to wear him."

She clipped the thermal charge to her belt and primed it. The charge was not a bomb so much as a fulcrum; she had only one lever left.

"You run," she said.

Ava wet her lips, tears forming constellations on her cheeks. "I run."

They had been given only the smallest of bargains: carry memory away, let something else keep the soil. Lauren rose to meet the creature like a single woman sent into a myth to close the book.

There was a brief, human ritual before the end that had nothing to do with ceremony and everything to do with keeping a promise. Lauren, with fingers that trembled, smoothed the frayed tag on the shard and, for an instant, looked at the two moons through a fissure in the reactor casing. "Candle-ready," she muttered—the silly, private phrase they had used on launch when they set a cylinder on the table and pretended it would warm a room someday. Lauren had meant it as a comfort then; now it was an order to the stubbornness of memory.

The spore-being reached for her with arms that were not entirely arms; its face settled on Markus in something like hope. It gathered her in a gesture that might have been gentle if anything about that moment could be gentle.

Lauren pressed the detonator to its composite heart.

White fire unstitched the reactor. The ship screamed—metal wrenched, atmospheres expelled, grav-fields stuttered. Shockwaves threw them like rag-dolls through corridors that had only moments before been home. The spore-being and Lauren burned together into a furnace of light.

Ava ran.

She stumbled through a ruin of collapses, through hallways that tilted at impossible angles and panels that wept living light. Small details engraved themselves on her mind in the motion: the pop of a nanobulb as it imploded, the smell of copper and lemon oil in a recess where Isaac had once left a thermos, the echo of Spacy’s avatar voice breaking into a near-human phrase she could not quite place.

Somewhere overhead Spacy hiccupped and tried to say more. "Hope… preserved… transmission queued… goodbye…"

Then the avatar pixelated into nothing.

Ava reached the escape pods. The one she picked was half-crushed and slick with condensate, but it sealed and held. The hatch fought the hydraulic actuation; a spring pin caught and snapped like a finger being closed on. She slammed into the seat, lungs burning, and punched emergency ejection without the courtesy of reading guidance tracks. The pod bit outward and tumbled through Zild’s thick sky, past spore-laden storms and two blurred moons hanging like watchful eyes.

In the pod the shard lay against her sternum. She pressed it there until its corners dug into the skin and thought of Lena’s last smile, of Markus’ grin transfigured, of Isaac’s slow, private hum about the warm cylinder, of the Ark’s hymn as it ended. She kept repeating—an act like recitation or a superstition—every name she could hold. The names were ballast.

Outside, the Ark shrieked its dying cry. Its silhouette sloughed off hot, living sections that fell into the planet’s dark like so many burned leaves. In the distance, where the ship had once been proud and whole, Zild continued its patient work of reintegration. The duct-beat folded into planetary noise until it was indistinguishable from wind over root.

The transmission queued from Spacy’s last processes seeped outward on a narrow beam, a packet stitched from their archive and their will. It was brief and human in cadence, the kind of simple elegy they had practiced composing in their head for years in case something needed saying before a door closed:

We lived. We fought. We remembered.

Ava let that loop in her head. She did not know what would come of the shard, of the coordinates lodged inside it, of the archives and Shale’s notes. She only knew she had something to carry. She held the small object like a promise and, for the first time since the duct-beat began, tried to imagine a way of saying the story to someone who might listen.

Below, Zild sang its patient, ancient song. Above, the pod tumbled on and the two moons watched silently as a small, stubborn thread of human memory unspooled into the dark.
