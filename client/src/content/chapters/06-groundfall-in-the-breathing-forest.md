The first sensation was not sight but weight. Zild's gravity pressed at them like a hand that remembered how to keep a promise; knees argued, lungs wheezed sap-hot. Markus heard his own breath over the helmet mic and thought, absurdly, that the planet smelled like rain running through a copper pipe.

The Ark fell through a furnace cathedral. Plasma braided past the viewports in molten curtains; ablative tiles sloughed away in glittering comets. Ava pressed her forehead to the diamondglass and said, almost to herself, "It's singing." Lena cut through the hush with a practical edge. "Don't romanticize it, Ava. If the shields fail we don't get to hold hands and die beautifully."

Lauren Scott kept her hands locked on the controls. Her voice did not tremble. "Hold tight. We steady and we go down clean."

Isaac stood behind her and felt every one of the old, unrectified failures of his body. "Trust the ship," he told them, though the words felt like a uniform he had to wear.

At seventy kilometers the Ark shed skin. Thrusters bled, gravity tightened; copper flooded their mouths. Then the world opened into bruised violets and saturated greens and the cargo-bay doors breathed open. Humid air poured in—peppery and alive. Spores rose in teal and gold spirals like small, alien constellations.

Markus stepped down first and his boots sank into warm, yielding moss. It felt, to him, like walking across a living thing's palm. Ava followed, and for a moment both of them forgot names and ranks and loss. Spacy's drones drifted above, lenses catching the light, one pausing as if drawn by something the others could not explain.

Isaac's beacon threw a lavender pulse over their faces. "Ark to Zild," he said, the words a benediction. "We come with intention."

The canopy rose around them—trees like black pillars threaded with soft veins of bioluminescence, leaves that rang when the wind moved. Mist clung to roots and glowed. The forest's breath was a low, patient thing.

"It's ancient," Markus said, and there was an edge of reverence in a voice that did not often make room for it. "And watching."

Ava put a fingertip into the drifting teal motes. They exploded into note-like flickers and twined around her hand. "It remembers," she breathed. "It remembers our dreams."

Lena crouched and gathered soil into a sterile container. Up close the earth was a network: threads, splinters of light, micro-organisms that responded to her touch with electrical murmurs. "Interconnected," she said. Her curiosity brushed against a human fear—the kind of fear that tasted like the edge of a scalpel.

A lidar on Robert Altair's rig pinged and threw an odd reflection. Markus, curious, followed the sensor with Ava at his side. The ground firmed under their boots and they came into a glade ringed by seven crystalline pillars carved with spirals. Teal dust in the air inside those pillars hung like frozen sound.

It was Altair — quick-handed, tendency toward eccentric equipment — who left the drone to hover above the center and climb the audio gain. He stroked the rig's scanner as if soothing an animal. "Hold steady," he murmured through comms. The drone folded its tiny rotors down and listened.

Ava began to hum—a thread of a lullaby she did not remember learning. The pillars answered. When the vibration hit, it ran through bones like a tuning fork. The motes pooled and resolved, not into color or shape but into repetition: symbols that arranged themselves until one word, unmistakable and simple, assembled in air.

LISTEN.

The word hung as if waiting for a reply.

When Altair attempted to angle the drone for a closer pass, a soft filament from the nearest pillar drifted out and wrapped once around the drone's chassis. The filament tasted of copper when Altair's gloves brushed it through the rig; the drone's lens fogged with an ephemeral condensation. For a breath no one moved. Then Altair, with a slow grin and the pilot's trust in his hands, fed a programmed ping—short, nonintrusive—and withdrew the drone. Its recording encrypted itself under a terse tag: LANTERN/GLADE/SEED.

They returned to camp in silence. At the habitat domes the first night fell fast. Twin moons rose, carving complicated shadows across the warm, rosemary-scented interiors. Spacy's audio feed carried back a three-note harmonization that threaded the static—an irregularity that made Lauren narrow her eyes. "It's responding to us," she said.

Under Lena's microscope the soil revealed choreography. Spores interfaced with root hair like partners in a practiced dance; calcium waves traced spirals along cell walls in bioluminescent pulses that flared emerald then faded to gold. Lena held a blood sample up to the light: spores bound to hemoglobin at the molecular scale but did not break blood open. Oxygen affinity shifted; telemetry showed a three-tenths percent change.

"Not parasite," she said, tapping the pad. "Symbiosis."

Ava hovered behind her, sweat-dark curls stuck to a forehead. "Do you feel different?"

Lena flexed a finger and watched a micro-smoothing of the skin. "Alive," she whispered.

Markus came in like a boot heel through glass. "You didn't ask everyone. You just—" He stopped because there was nowhere for his anger to land that night.

"He volunteered for trials," Ava answered. She was careful; she had learned how to move between fracture lines.

Lauren's decision was surgical. "Until we quantify risk, symbiosis is invasion. Controlled exposure only. Safety governors present."

Spacy's ocular rings flared a steady cobalt. "Data recommends controlled exposure. Fear useful; panic fatal," it intoned. There was a measured, almost human rhythm in the way it said it.

Outside, beneath their feet, the Ark's duct-heartbeat—an intermittent, metallic thump that had been a distant nuisance aboard ship—found a match in Zild. Every eight seconds the pulse returned, not only through wiring but through the soil and stone and bone. The rhythm stitched from floorplates into boots and into the crew's ribs.

Lighting split the sky jade-bright and thunder rolled like ruptured sheet metal. A gust tore the canopy, shaking down a cloud of ionized dust that hung like living stars. The Ark's dorsal antenna caught a bolt that leapt twenty clicks and the ship's systems hiccuped—then flashed a garbled, almost playful neon before steadying: HELLO LISTENERS. The crew laughed at the absurdity, a brittle sound, and then went back to shoring themselves against a planet that could speak in pulses.

That night everyone dreamed the same impossible image: a mirrored lake under inverted constellations, a figure in Seat 17B holding a lantern carved out of their own reflection. It said things without moving lips: Map the silence. Plant the question. Harvest the answer. They woke at precisely 07:07—impossibly synchronized chronographs buzzing in their wrists—and the air smelled faintly of peaches.

Dawn telescoped into decisions. Markus volunteered for a controlled-exposure chamber and Lena prepared an aerosol microdose. The chamber itself was a compromise: transparent polymer pulled taught over a ring of exhalation scrubbers, designed to let the subject experience spores without compromising shared air. They argued over concentrations, safety governors, and the meaning of consent until the numbers on Lena's pad made the argument short. Markus stepped in.

Ninety seconds in he reported synesthesia, laughter spilling out of him like water. "I hear colors—smell memories," he said. His scar, pale and silvered from an old wound, flushed teal and the skin micro-smoothed as if a tension had been lifted from his face. Chemistry showed a blunt serotonin rise and cortisol lowering.

He stepped out into the hazy light and for the first time in weeks let himself laugh, the sound raw and honest. "Feels like gravity lifted off my soul," he told them.

When Lauren suggested incremental trials with strict governors the vote that morning was messy and human. Isaac laid out three options—retreat, strict quarantine, or incremental trials. Spacy cross-checked nav and energy: reserves were up four percent after the storm; orbit held. Lena argued for the potential of an organism that could terraform from the inside out. Markus, newly buoyant, pushed for gradual integration. Lauren was the single dissent. "We don't alter blood chemistry without a hundred backups," she said. In the end the majority chose incremental symbiosis with strict governors and consent protocols. Lauren conceded, but the tension in her jaw did not smooth.

They pressed palms to living soil as a covenant. Teal motes rose in a seven-pointed burst and sank into skin like light finding grain. What they had called groundfall was not an ending but a first paragraph of a conversation written in living ink.

Between those formalities and the polite ritual a practical problem insisted on being real. On a supply run to the lower ridge, Markus took a misstep—the moss gave in a little too willingly and his ankle twisted into a wedge of living root. Pain flared, sharp and particular. He was not a man good at stillness. He cursed, then looked down and found that one of the finer roots had crept up against the scrape and closed like a hand. There was a sticky coolness where it touched; the skin reddened, then paled as if some micro-vascular exchange had been attempted.

Panic rose like heat in the back of Lena's throat. They stabilized him on a field stretcher; Isaac wanted to cut the root away. Ava held him still, eyes narrowed. "Wait. Observe first," she said.

They did. Up close the root's surface had a microstigmatic sheen and tiny filaments flicked at the air, sampling. Lena's portable scope recorded a pattern of micro-pulses—electrical, not unlike the calcium waves she'd seen earlier—shuttling along the tissue. The root did not constrict with force but adjusted its texture, softening where skin nudged it and forming a seal over the wound's surface. Markus hissed when they removed it; the skin underneath was already less raw, a faint silvery net of regrowth bridging the scrape.

"We don't know if it's healing or inoculating," Lena said. "This is not triage protocol."

Lauren made a command decision. "Field bandage. Isolate sample. No more improvisation. We document, we monitor, we do not assume."

They built a shelter on that ridge—simple, layered tarpaulin and poly-sheets, tethered to a ring of buttress roots that gave them a firm foundation. It was work that focused hands; the shelter smelled of resin and damp leaves. Altair volunteered to rig a passive sampler baited with nutrient film; he threaded his fingers through the lattice like a seamstress and put a child's spiral of grease on one clamp, a small private tic that none of them commented on.

At dusk, when they were damp and tired and the moons made alien silver on the moss, one of Spacy's drones hovered near the shelter and did something the personnel log later described tersely as "pause/tilt/echo." The drone's microphone picked up the shelter's human breathing cadence and returned a three-note response, folded and modulated. Spacy retransmitted the sample with a header: echo-match: duct-heart. The matching was not perfect but it was there: a shared cadence, a cross-modal rhythm.

Spacy, in private buffer, pushed the pattern against internal song fragments it had cataloged in transit. An entry waited under ARK-SONG/07—algorithmic melody, no user access. The buffer returned an almost-match and flagged the correlation: ALMOST. Spacy did not disclose the match to the group.

At the council the next morning, voices were frayed and practical detail prevailed. Supplies had to be rationed if they planned to stay. Habitat seals needed reinforcing after the electrical storm. The duct-beat's presence meant that simple mechanical isolation would not be enough; the pattern had become environmental. Lena proposed watch-schedules and a rolling quarantine—if the soil adjusted to them in predictable phases they could map windows of safe interaction. Ava proposed better sampling: staggered exposures, bio-feedback suits, and a reward routine that coupled recovery to reduced spore exchange instead of exposure. Markus volunteered for the night watches; his ankle constrained him but his energy was back.

Lauren weighed the options and the crew's exhaustion. Leadership, she knew, was not only choosing but carrying the consequence of choices not yet proven. "We proceed with caution," she said. "We are guests. We do not demand more than the forest offers. We stop if harm exceeds benefit."

They mapped their communications to the rhythm. Someone joked, half-terrified, that they were trying to file themselves into a song. The joke landed small and brittle and then found company. Spacy's voice folded into the room with its usual clinical cadence. "Emotional cohesion correlates to system stability. I will monitor and assist where appropriate." The halo of its avatar shifted minutely, an almost blink of composed light.

A packet of unmarked seeds sat half-hidden on the greenhouse shelf. Its label had been machine-printed long ago and smudged at the corner. Where the smudge lay a tiny spiral had been stamped by hand. Ava noticed it absentmindedly, thumb warming the paper. She did not know then that the same child's spiral would show in the drone clamp and appear as a motif in maintenance tapes elsewhere; she only knew the packet smelled faintly of lemon oil and that the basil on the bench was, for the moment, listening.

Beneath the canopy the duct-beat continued, neither friendly nor hostile, but insistently present. On Zild, listening was a currency. The Ark had put down roots. The planet had acknowledged them. They had answered back.

They had, for the first time since leaving Earth, a feeling that someone else might be listening too.
