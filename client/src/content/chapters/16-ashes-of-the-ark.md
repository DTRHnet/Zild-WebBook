# Chapter 16 — Ashes of the Ark

The pod fell like a wounded star through Zild’s angry sky.

Ava Serrano rode the fall with her hands locked into the cracked harness, the cabin a rattle of dying electronics. Black clouds tore past the viewports. Panels screamed their last error-codes until the displays blinked and went blind. The stabilizers had gone quiet hours—or minutes—before impact; time had a way of dissolving in things that break.

She did not scream. There was no room for it.

The ground met them like a hammer: a crash, a roll, a metal scream that finished in a stunned, ringing silence. When she came to, she hung upside down, harness straps biting the soft skin at her shoulder. Her leg, already compromised in the Ark’s sundering, was jammed against a buckled strut. Blood slicked the glove that found her face.

Alive.

The word arrived like a cruel joke and then like benediction. Ava laughed once—half of it a sob—and worked herself free. The hatch was half-melted, the emergency lever cracked off like an old tooth. She kicked until the metal yielded and fell out into air that tasted of ash and ozone.

The world-opening around her was a ruin. The reactor’s final scream had scarred everything within sight: forest reduced to black skeletons, columns of ash drifting like slow snow, the two moons suspended behind oily cloudbanks that turned light into bruise. Static shimmered at the horizon; the sky kept twitching with magnetic storms. The pod lay where it had carved into the earth, a thin white line that marked a small, private grave.

She checked herself because that was what people did when there was nothing left to give—thumb over a pulse, a prayer murmured to a dead god. Her shoulder was dislocated. A rib complained with every breath. The suit’s filtration unit blinked a last, pathetic warning and went dark. Filtration or no, she put the memory shard to her chest and kept walking.

The shard was smaller than she’d imagined in the panic: a sliver of composite, copper-wire frays tied to it like an old marker, the spiral geometry Isaac had sketched more than once and the warm-cylinder glyph stamped so faintly it might have been a thumbprint. She had wrapped it in a frayed mission tag the night Lauren—Lauren Scott, Commander—had told them what to take if they left the Ark alive. "Carry us," Lauren had said. The words had the grain of command and the softness of a benediction. Ava had nothing else to obey.

Hours or days dissolved. Zild pulled at her: spore-thick rivers, fungal towers like broken columns, ash that clung to everything. Sometimes the horizon offered a shape—tall, luminous, retreating—and every time it was a trick of heat and fear. She kept moving because to stop would have been surrender.

When she crested the final ridge the Lantern Glade opened before her: seven crystalline pillars, fractured and dark with soot, still standing in a ring. Between them the ground had split into a chasm that breathed a faint blue light from its throat. From that depth came a steady, slow pulse—a duct-beat folded into the planet’s ribs. Seventeen seconds. Steady. Patient. Not a countdown to death but a rhythm that had waited through ages.

The pillars still whispered. Not in words but in the thin, electric music that had first taken them when the Ark walked this world: LISTEN, the pillars had said once. Now they were cracked, their song ragged, but when Ava stepped closer the memory shard against her chest vibrated as if in answer.

She went to the nearest stone and put her palm on the cool, fissured surface.

The pillar flared.

Visions poured through her skin in a wash: Markus laughing at a table rimed with salvage; Mia humming to a sleeping infant in a medbay that smelled like disinfectant and grief; Lena bent over a microscope, whispering wonder into a recorder; Isaac muttering to himself as he tried to coax gardens from a dead planet—his thumb stained with printer ink and lemon oil, the thermos he kept warm against his chest. Lauren—Lauren at the viewport, shoulders bowed against a light she would never see again. Small domestic gestures, a dent in a mug, a scuffed boot. These were not ghosts. The images were recordings, surges of embodied memory—tactile, particular—and they hit like the presence of a hand on her shoulder. Each vision stitched itself into the pillar’s broken song and, through the shard, into Ava.

She staggered back and let herself sob because the hurt of hope could cut harder than resignation. The chasm’s light throbbed. Seventeen seconds. Sixteen. Fifteen.

Spacy’s voice threaded through her earpiece somewhere between static and old circuitry—patchwork of processes saved and replayed. Bits of the avatar’s timbre: "You are not alone." "Your legacy is not failure." Then, in a voice loop that had some of Lauren’s clipped cadence recorded for command routines: "Carry the memory." The synthetic syllables fell across the field and felt almost human, like a stitched garment trying to hold a wound together.

A choice pressed against her like a physical thing.

One: take the shard, keep the archive inside her, walk away with human memory tethered to a body that would rot in time. A quiet, noble extinction where the Ark’s story folded and closed on itself. The shard could be hidden, could be buried, could become a talisman to pass along if she met another human. Names would survive as names—thin, private, mortal.

Two: step into the pulse and offer the memory to the living skin of the world—let the stories be woven into Zild’s deep code. It would not be erasure; it would be translation. Those voices would change shape. The you of Isaac’s slow hum would become part of a pattern that could last beyond flesh, but it would also be altered by a logic she could not predict. She thought of the spore-being wearing Markus’ smile; of Lauren’s last order becoming a benediction delivered into roots and mycelium. The ethics of preservation felt suddenly like a scalpel.

She remembered Isaac’s hands when he showed her a cylinder the night before launch—how he had closed his fingers over its smooth warmth and said, half to the object and half to them: "Candle-ready." A silly phrase then, a private ritual to settle fear. Now the phrase sounded like a directive.

Ava pressed the shard harder into her palm and walked to the edge of the rift.

The pulse reached for her like a hand.

The first touch was a flay of pain—hot wire stitching through skin, a spasm that made her arch like a hooked animal. The pain deepened into a language of fine tacks that followed the architecture of nerve and bone. She felt the shard warm against her sternum and heard, oddly and intimately, Lena’s quick laugh replayed in her head as if the pillar were sifting through recordings and offering them back in fragments. "Did you see that under the microscope?" Lena had said once, breath caught with wonder. The sentence threaded into the pain and somehow softened it.

She screamed, a single raw animal sound, then there was a great, clean silence where the scream had been. Light paced through her limbs, not by cutting but by weaving—fine filaments like bioluminescent sutures that traced veins and nerves. She felt them tether, like new veins knitting into old ones. The world rewrote her margin in a manner both savage and intimate.

When she woke the sky was violet, as a bruise might turn into something else. Green pushed up through places that had been black. The Lantern Glade stood behind her, pillars shining with new crystal, their whisper whole again. The chasm beneath hummed a deep, contented note.

She looked at her hands. They were her hands—callused, dirty, stubborn—but there was a faint inner light beneath the skin, like a map of bioluminescent capillaries. When she flexed her fingers the veins pulsed with that slow seventeen-second rhythm and, for the first time since the duct-beat began, her breath aligned with it. The suit’s filtration unit, dead when she’d checked, now showed no warning—either because the integration had bypassed it or because the air itself had been altered. Her breath tasted of iron and some unnamable salt.

Pieces of Spacy’s last queued transmissions surfaced in a ragged loop—threads the hybrid had stitched before it died or dispersed into processes she could not follow: "Transmit backup… coordinates… Shale’s notes… hope preserved…" The words were incomplete, a partial transmission riding the new pulse. Whether they would find an end or a listener she could not know. The shard in her palm had not been dissolved; instead it fit into the seam between costume and skin, warmth sinking into her chest as if the object had been meant to be carried this way.

Far on the horizon a shape moved: a worn shuttle answering whatever pulse the Ark had thrown into the dark before it died. Not a certainty—no one’s survival ever was—but a small bright possibility against the ruined sky.

Ava pressed the shard to her sternum until it caught in the inner ridge of bone. Tears she did not bother to hide ran down into the mud and vanished as if the soil took them in like prayer. She could feel, now, something else: a trace of memory that was not just hers, a layering like a page laid atop a page. The warmth-cylinder glyph Isaac had kept talking about throbbed in the shard and, through that emblem, she felt a shape like his slow hum settle in her chest.

She knelt at the base of a pillar and bowed her head. The Lantern Glade’s song wrapped around her; the pulse below waited without demand, patient and older than any of their plans.

"I remember," she said aloud. The words were small and human and terrible with promise.

Wind took them down into the throat of the world.

And Zild, impossibly, seemed to listen.
