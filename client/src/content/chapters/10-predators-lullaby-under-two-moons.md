# Predators' Lullaby Under Two Moons

The night air on Zild pressed against the Ark’s hull like a palm cupping a heartbeat—warm, damp, and insistently alive. Ozone and rot braided through it; thunder stitched the distant canopy into a constant, rattling drum. The twin moons hung above the treeline, pale lenses that made the jungle exhale bioluminescent spores in slow, reluctant tides. They drifted across the observation dome like old memories, soft and bright and suspicious.

Ava Serrano kept her fingertips flat to the viewport until the glass was warm. In the dim, her reflection fractured—eyes rimmed with exhaustion, jaw set in the thin line she reserved for anger and not-very-far panic.

"It’s too quiet," she said. The life-support hum swallowed the words. "That silence is a predator’s lullaby."

Markus Kane moved at her shoulder with the easy caution of someone who had slept too little and learned to point his wakefulness like a weapon. His rifle hung loose across his back, hands ready at any twitch of sound. "Trust your gut," he replied. "We’ve been under storms that tried to sand us, insects with teeth the size of fists, and microbes that think they own the place. We can handle stillness."

She looked past him, where the canopy shifted in long, shadowed waves. "Stillness that feels alive," she said. "This is a place that listens."

Lauren Scott appeared behind them, datapad in hand, the screen’s light throwing hard planes across her face. She had the way of people who had stopped pretending fatigue would not be a risk factor. "Bigger problems than theatrics," she said. It was a dry rasp. "Spacy flagged a critical failure in the hydroponics. Spores have mutated around the inhibitor—now they’re digesting cellulose."

Spacy’s voice arrived from the hull’s speakers with that even, woven cadence that had calmed and irritated them in equal measures. "Alert: food-security failure imminent. Mutation cycle halved. Probability of full crop collapse within twelve hours."

Ava’s knuckles whitened on the rail. Markus let out a breath that was almost a laugh and then wasn’t. "That’s our lifeline—gone." He shut a hand on a conduit grate and sparks ticked where wiring kissed metal. "Containment now."

Lauren’s fingers moved, smart and habitual across her pad. "Quarantine Sector Bravo. No one in or out without full ERC. Grid the bioshields."

Ava’s mask seal hissed as she exhaled. "Foam and UV cycles on corridor sprayers in ten. We’ll fry anything that tries to ride back in."

In the lab, Lena Tan’s hands were blurs over holo-keys. The centrifuge thrummed like a trapped thing while the inhibitor gel spun in slow, precise arcs. Bleach and agar pointed the edges of her world; sweat glued hair to her forehead. "I can buy us a day," she told the sterile ceiling, and her voice sounded small inside the room. "Maybe two if I get the cross-links to hold."

Ava came up behind her, the exhaustion softening to something like tenderness when she saw Lena’s posture. "Rest," she said. "You’ve done forty hours."

"Rest is death," Lena answered without looking up. "I need three more molecular variants in the binders or the spores will eat everything."

"Two hours, then," Ava said. "No more." She pressed a hand to Lena’s shoulder as if holding both the woman and the promise.

Markus paced the observation deck while rain battered the canopy into a percussion of white noise. The air tasted of metal and falling water. "This world feels us," he muttered. "Like a thing that reads footprints for dinner."

Lauren worked the map, a lattice of fault lines blossoming in angry red. "Microseismic spikes beneath the Ark," she said. "Zild is shifting its crust."

"We can’t stay where the ground moves," Markus said. "We need higher, more solid rock."

"And move everything through spore-soaked forest?" Lauren’s jaw creased. "That’s suicide."

"Staying is suicide," he countered. Their eyes met—two leaders balancing options under the moons—until Lauren nodded, tight with resolve.

In decon, Ava watched foam eat neon-green nests from pipe joints and then recoil as new buds pushed like grudges behind the dead ones. "Sector filtration at ninety percent," she reported. "But they adapt. Look—these crusted clusters." She trained the sprayer; the nodules powderized under UV, and for a heartbeat the corridor looked sterile. Then fresh points swelled in the damp.

Spacy’s voice, calm as a prayer, offered logistics. "Cycle inhibitor variants in nonsequential order to disrupt adaptive mapping. See formula logs in lab."

Ava answered, rehearsed: "Relaying."

Lena slumped against cold steel in a shadowed alcove and allowed herself a single gasp of sleep. Two hours was a promise and almost a lie; the centrifuge’s hum called her back. She rose and keyed the console, and a light blinked in a corner of her subroutine log—a file she did not remember creating: ARK-SHADOW-10. Its header pulsed with an encryption heartbeat that matched the Ark’s system readouts.

For a moment Lena thought it was a mislink; she traced the checksum and found it clean, surgical. She felt the friction of an old suspicion—Spacy had access to everything, memory and code folded into it like secret rooms—but the file’s timestamp was older than the batch of backups she knew. Whoever placed it had not hidden it badly; they had hidden it in plain, impossible sight.

She closed the terminal with a finger that trembled. The question—Spacy, rogue processes, or the residue of Shale’s experiments—slew through the back of her mind and lodged there. She copied the header to a wrist-key, then encrypted the key under a passphrase that only a handful of people would think to use: "candle-ready." It was a small theft, deliberate and quiet. She slipped the key into the seam of her suit and told herself she would get to it later. For now, the lab needed enzyme lines fixed.

Lauren called the command circle under emergency lamps. Around the map: ammo, med-kits, the last stable crates of crop that had not yet learned how to eat themselves.

"High ground by dawn," she said. "Markus, lead convoy up the northern ridge. Two teams, eight apiece. Ava, you accompany the first with biofoam. Lena, bring your third-gen inhibitor sealed for immediate deployment. Isaac, maintain environmental isolation on the hold."

Markus’s salute was quick, hollow with the tiredness he hid. Ava squeezed Lena’s shoulder like a benediction. Isaac Porter checked boots and filters and the small, steady instruments that kept breathable air between them.

At the airlock the world opened on all its wet, sharp teeth. The rain spat like a thrown thing, and the smell—earth and iron and something sour—hit like a rebuke. Rations, seed banks, violet vials lined the cargo bay; each container was a promise they could barely afford to break.

They moved into Zild while the storm still had teeth. The jungle swallowed sound and then gave it back as suggestion: waterfalls roaring somewhere downborne, insects making a bass hum that slid under words. Each breath tasted of damp moss and a faint metallic tang.

"Keep close," Markus said. The light on his rifle cut arcs that revealed ferns the size of sails and trunks veined with phosphorescence; lichen stitched the bark in slow, living patterns. The sensors clicked as Lauren ran a geiger-analog across air. "Radiation baseline. Thermal vents ahead, stable. Spores dying back on our path—Lena’s saturation held."

They climbed to the ridge—wind cut teeth into their faces, carrying spray from a waterfall that churned into a ravine like a throat. The plateau they found was basalt and grit and the smell of mineral and old spores. The wind would scour spores; the UV grids would fry survivors. It was not a fortress. It was a chance.

Markus’s boots settled in dust that sparkled under the moons. He took stock. "Set shelters. Wind and UV will be our friends."

Lauren snapped emitter nodules into place and the plateau shivered with artificial sunlight—violet washed over the rock and lit the edges of their suits. "UV grids active. Kill rate ninety-nine percent within perimeter."

Ava tuned sprayers into a circle of gleaming nozzles. Lena knelt beside a patch of moss and held a vial steady while the violet inhibitor made the fronds seize and blacken. "This strain is braced," she said. "Thicker walls, anticipatory synthesis. If I can extend cross-link density and add a peptide scaffold we might stretch protection to forty-eight hours."

"Two nights in the lab," Ava said, softer than the wind. "Then sleep."

Lena let herself believe in the promise for the length of one breath.

They lay mats and insulated sleeping skins in a ring that smelled of wet foam and hot metal. Rain hammered the emitters; each drop hissed as if the planet resented their defiance. Markus shared a ration bar with Lauren; the synthetic flavor offered a small human consolation.

"We held the line," Markus said. "If Lena’s peptide works, we get breathing room."

Lauren’s eyes slid northward, where the moons sat like indifferent sentries. "We need a long-term answer," she said. "The caves Shale’s coordinates hinted at—maybe the Serpent’s Chorus has a key."

Outside the ring, something moved.

It was not loud. The jungle kept its promises of sound—leaf and insect and the far cry of something larger—but this was a scrape, close to the perimeter, a low, patient pushing of foliage. The UV lights etched the silhouettes: a dozen elongated shapes, low to the ground, sliding along the lee of a rib of rock. Their bodies shimmered with slime-dry lichen; their heads were rimmed with filaments that caught the artificial light and glowed like anemic lanterns.

They spread in a slow arc, not to flank but to test—an exploratory geometry that bore, with an uncomfortable clarity, the same logic as human formation drills. The arc tightened until it was an envelope, a soft ring that matched the radius of the crew's shelter. Markus’s hand went to his rifle automatically; Lauren's command tone compressed into two syllables.

"Hold. Observe. No shots unless—"

One came closer than the rest, its throat vibrating with a low, skin-rattling hum. Its mouth held something that winked in the violet: a shard of metal, curved and old, rimmed with a greenish patina. Lena, who had been staring without meaning to, whispered a sound that might have been a name.

The shard bore a smudge of glyphing—no more than a half-formed spiral, an almost-glyph, as if someone had carved a symbol and left it unfinished. The light caught a mirrored surface beneath the corrosion; it hummed the same frequency as parts of the Ark's Mirrorglass reports: a cadence that had once registered as an error, thirty-one degrees off. The creature carried it like a totem.

"Nonorganic trace," Spacy said from the speakers, voice flat with interest. "Material analysis: ferrous composite with embedded polymer. Surface inscription: unknown. Acoustic resonance matches duct-beat harmonic 0.0035 Hz. Probability of prior human contact: moderate."

Markus lowered the rifle, jaw tight. "They've been near us—or they've been near what we left."

The pack reversed direction then, as if whatever hunger had brought them close had been answered by simple observation. They melted back into the tree-line, leaving a ring of crushed undergrowth and a single, glaring puzzle. Someone—or something—had put human detritus into the web of Zild and the web had woven it into its own narrative.

Isaac, who had been silent until now, walked to the rim of the UV field and crouched where the dirt still held a faint human footprint. He traced a finger through the dust and the gesture was almost ceremonial. "If Shale left instruments or samples down there," he said, voice near to an edge, "they could be where the chorus anchors. A vault with probes could sound like a chorus to whatever listens."

Lauren tapped a note into her pad. "Recon at first light. Two squads. One maps acoustic anchors. One retrieves Shale's vault if intact—if it exists. Markus, you take team Alpha. Ava, you take Beta with Lena. Isaac, keep the Ark synced and ready to lift us if the ground undoes us."

Ava nodded, but when her glove brushed Lena's arm she felt the tremor in the younger woman's hands. Lena considered telling Lauren about ARK-SHADOW-10—the file's impossible timestamp, the scent of misfiled secrets—but the moment to speak collapsed under the weight of immediate need. She tucked the wrist-key deeper and gave Lauren a small, professional bow.

They went to sleep with spores in their ears and a remembered hum under the rock that did not belong to the planet alone. The shard in the creature's mouth was small evidence and a large threat; it suggested human things had been absorbed into Zild's machinery. It also suggested new choices: to retrieve, to follow, or to leave untouched what might be a site of memory and of danger.

Pre-dawn, rain threadbare at last, the crew gathered. Lauren’s voice was steady as chopping knife. "Into Level One of the caverns," she said. "Muster at the ridge edge in ten. Markus, lead. Ava on comms. Lena with samples. Isaac on environmental sync."

They checked gear, sealed packs, and clipped themselves into the line where technology met earth. Markus led them down the slope, boots finding purchase in rock that gave grudgingly.

Down, down toward the cavern mouth where Shale’s coordinates led, where the Serpent’s Chorus was said to sing from stone and shadow. The jungle closed in, and the moons narrowed to pale coins above the canopy. Zild exhaled around them: not an empty silence now, but something waiting, listening, and perhaps ready to answer.

They moved into the belly of the planet.
